From Boltzmann Statistics to Fluid Dynamics

Section titled “From Boltzmann Statistics to Fluid Dynamics”

🚨 This section is a reproduction of From Boltzmann Statistics to Fluid Dynamics

For simplicity, this simulation works in two dimensions, and discretizes space into a square grid or lattice. Each lattice site is big enough to hold a large number of gas molecules, which will be moving in various directions due to their thermal motion plus any large-scale fluid flow.

We approximate this rich complexity by allowing only nine different velocity vectors only, corresponding to nine elementary displacements during a unit of time: up, down, right, left, along any 45° diagonal,or zero (staying in place). The nine elementary displacements are illustrated and listed below, using a unit system in which the lattice spacing is one unit.

e0→=0\overrightarrow{ e_0} = 0e0​​=0
e1→=(1,0)\overrightarrow{ e_1} = (1,0)e1​​=(1,0)
e2→=(0,1)\overrightarrow{ e_2} = (0,1)e2​​=(0,1)
e3→=(−1,0)\overrightarrow{ e_3} = (-1,0)e3​​=(−1,0)
e4→=(0,−1)\overrightarrow{ e_4} = (0,-1)e4​​=(0,−1)
e5→=(1,1)\overrightarrow{ e_5} = (1,1)e5​​=(1,1)
e6→=(−1,1)\overrightarrow{ e_6} = (-1,1)e6​​=(−1,1)
e7→=(−1,−1)\overrightarrow{ e_7} = (-1,-1)e7​​=(−1,−1)
e8→=(1,−1)\overrightarrow{ e_8} = (1,-1)e8​​=(1,−1)

To keep track of how many gas molecules at a site are moving in each of these nine directions, we’ll need nine variables. I’ll call these variables nin_ini​, for i=0…8i=0\ldots8i=0…8, and define them as the respective densities of molecules with the corresponding velocities. Then the total density ρ\rhoρ of all molecules at any given site is their sum:

ρ=∑i=08ni.\rho = \sum_{i=0}^8 n_i.ρ=i=0∑8​ni​.

We can also calculate the macroscopic fluid flow velocity, by taking a weighted average of the nine elementary velocities. I’ll call the flow velocity u→\overrightarrow{ u}u, and use the symbol ccc for the natural unit of velocity, one grid site per unit time. Then the flow velocity at any given site is

u→=∑i=08niρei→c,\overrightarrow{ u} = \sum_{i=0}^8 \frac{n_i}{\rho} \overrightarrow{ e_i} c,u=i=0∑8​ρni​​ei​​c,

which we can write more explicitly as

ux=cρ(n1+n5+n8−n3−n6−n7)uy=cρ(n2+n5+n6−n4−n7−n8)\begin{array}{ll} u_x &=& \frac{c}{\rho}(n_1 + n_5 + n_8 - n_3 - n_6 - n_7) \\ u_y &=& \frac{c}{\rho}(n_2 + n_5 + n_6 - n_4 - n_7 - n_8) \end{array}ux​uy​​==​ρc​(n1​+n5​+n8​−n3​−n6​−n7​)ρc​(n2​+n5​+n6​−n4​−n7​−n8​)​

taking the xxx and yyy directions to point rightward and upward, respectively.

Thermal Velocities

Section titled “Thermal Velocities”

The thermal velocities of ideal gas molecules are described by the Boltzmann distribution, which in two dimensions is

D(v→)=m2πkTe−m∣v→∣2/2kT.D(\overrightarrow{ v}) = \frac{m}{2\pi kT} e^{-m|\overrightarrow{ v}|^2/2kT}.D(v)=2πkTm​e−m∣v∣2/2kT.

This is the function that, when integrated over any range of vxv_xvx​ and vyv_yvy​ values, gives the probability of a molecule’s velocity being in that range, where mmm is the molecule’s mass, kkk is Boltzmann’s constant, and TTT is the temperature. The exponent in the last equation is the molecule’s kinetic energy, and the prefactor m/2πkTm/2\pi kTm/2πkT ensures that the integral of D(v→)D(\overrightarrow{ v})D(v) over all velocity vectors equals 111 (which you can easily check).
