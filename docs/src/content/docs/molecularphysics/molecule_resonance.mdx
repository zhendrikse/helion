<div class="header_line"></div>

### Greenhouse effect of carbon dioxide
<div class="header_line"></div>

Why is carbon dioxide so important to global warming?

The global warming / greenhouse effect is mainly due to particular resonance of the carbon dioxide molecules.
In this animation, a carbon dioxide molecule is exposed to an alternating electric field.
Discover which frequencies the molecule responds to and if so, how.

### Model and implementation

<div class="header_line"></div>

The carbon dioxide and water molecules are modeled as a three-body 
system consisting of one central and two outer atoms.
For carbon dioxide, the equilibrium configuration is linear, 
with the carbon atom between the two oxygen atoms.

For the bond potential, we use Hooke's law:

$$
\overrightarrow{F}=-k\left(r-r_0\right)\hat{\mathbf{r}}
$$

where $r$ is the current bond length and $r_0$ is the equilibrium bond length.

For the angular interaction, let

$$
\overrightarrow{v}_1=\overrightarrow{r}_{outer_1}-
\overrightarrow{r}_{central}
$$

and

$$
\overrightarrow{v}_2 = \overrightarrow{r}_{outer_2}-
\overrightarrow{r}_{central}
$$

Because the equilibrium configuration is linear, the angular displacement is 
measured as the deviation from a straight line:

$$
\theta = \arccos
\left(
\frac{
\overrightarrow{v}_1\cdot(-\overrightarrow{v}_2)
}{
|\overrightarrow{v}_1| |\overrightarrow{v}_2|
}
\right)
$$

Thus, $\theta=0$ corresponds to a perfectly linear molecule. A bent molecule has $\theta>0$.

The direction of the bending force is determined from the sum of the two bond vectors:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{b}}=\frac{
\overrightarrow{v}_1+\overrightarrow{v}_2
}{
|\overrightarrow{v}_1+\overrightarrow{v}_2|
}
$$

The force applied to each outer atom is

$$
\boxed{
\overrightarrow{F}_{outer} = -k_t r_0\theta \hat{\mathbf{b}}
}
$$

while the central atom receives the compensating force

$$
\boxed{\overrightarrow{F}_{central} =-2\overrightarrow{F}_{outer}}
$$

The angular interaction is therefore a **three-body force**: the force on 
each atom depends on the positions of both bonds simultaneously. 
In the JavaScript implementation, the bending force is consequently 
applied to both bonds together, for example for carbon dioxide:

```js
co2.bond1.and(co2.bond2).apply(bendForce);
```

This implementation follows the force formulation used in the original VPython examples. 
In particular, for CO2 the angular displacement is defined as the deviation from 
linearity rather than as the difference from an equilibrium angle of $\pi$.

Finally, the interaction with the external electric field is modeled using the electric force

$$
\boxed{\overrightarrow{F} = q\overrightarrow{E}}
$$

where $q$ is the charge of the atom and $\overrightarrow{E}$ is the local electric field.

At each simulation step, the Coulomb, bond-stretching, and angular forces are first accumulated 
on all three atoms. Only after all forces have been applied are the atoms integrated forward in time.
